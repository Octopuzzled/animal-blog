Eight mysterious arms in the deep

The wondrous world of the octopus

Meet the octopus, a creature with blue blood, a brain that spills into its arms, and a talent for escaping any aquarium it finds boring. Grab a snack (not calamari, please) and let's explore the ocean's most delightful weirdo.

Author

I've been obsessed with octopuses since a documentary broke my brain in the best way. I untangle code by day and ocean trivia by night. If there's a tentacle in the room, I'll find it and probably name it.
Let me confess something: I'm not a marine biologist. I'm just someone who once watched an octopus open a jar from the inside and never fully recovered. If you've ever wondered why these animals are called the aliens of the sea, National Geographic's "Octopus 101" is the perfect crash course. Press play, then come back. I'll wait.

Okay, so you've seen it. Let's talk about what just happened. An octopus has three hearts: two pump blood through the gills, and the third takes care of the rest of the body. That blood is blue, thanks to a copper-based protein called hemocyanin. And here's my favorite part: the heart that serves the body actually pauses when the octopus swims. That's why they'd rather crawl. Honestly? Relatable. I also prefer not to do cardio every time I go out.

A few more facts that made me stare at my ceiling for a while:

  • Brains in the arms: An octopus has around 500 million neurons, and roughly two-thirds of them sit in its arms. Each arm can explore, taste, and react on its own, like eight very curious interns.
  • No bones, no problem: Its only hard part is a parrot-like beak. If the beak fits through a gap, the whole octopus does. Aquarium keepers have learned this the hard way.
  • Masters of disguise: Special skin cells let octopuses change color and texture in a fraction of a second, even though they're most likely colorblind. Yes, really. Nobody fully knows how they pull it off.

What gets me most is how short their lives are. Most octopus species live only one to two years, and many spend that time alone. No parents to teach them, no school, no YouTube tutorials. Everything they know about hunting, hiding, and problem-solving, they figure out on their own. And still they use tools, recognize individual humans, and sometimes squirt water at the ones they don't like. There are around 300 known species, from the tiny star-sucker pygmy octopus to the giant Pacific octopus, which can have an arm span of several meters. Some mimic poisonous fish to stay safe; others carry coconut shells around as mobile homes. Every time I read about them, I find one more reason to believe they're secretly running a very small, very wet civilization down there.

Pro tipps

  1. Want to see octopus intelligence in action? The documentary My Octopus Teacher is a gentle, beautiful follow-up to the video above. You'll find the trailer here. Bring tissues, just in case.
  2. Planning an aquarium visit? Go early or on a weekday. Octopuses are shy and often hide from crowds, so a quiet moment gives you the best chance to spot one. Bonus tip: it's "octopuses", not "octopi". The word comes from Greek, and now you can be insufferable at parties.
